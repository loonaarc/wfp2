# 2006 — Folke, "Resilience: The emergence of a perspective for social–ecological systems analyses"

## Citation

Carl Folke, "Resilience: The emergence of a perspective for social–ecological systems analyses", *Global Environmental Change*, Vol. 16, No. 3 (2006), pp. 253–267. DOI: 10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2006.04.002. Published by Elsevier Ltd. Author affiliation: Centre for Transdisciplinary Environmental Research (CTM), Department of Systems Ecology, Stockholm University, and the Beijer International Institute of Ecological Economics, Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences. (Article type: review / narrative overview.)

## Research Problem

The paper is a narrative history and synthesis, not an empirical study. It asks: what *is* the resilience perspective, where did it come from, and how has it developed into a framework for analysing linked social–ecological systems (SES)? Its central argument is that the dominant "stable equilibrium" / balance-of-nature view — which assumes a single steady state to which a disturbed system self-repairs — gives little insight into systems that are far from equilibrium. Folke contrasts this with a resilience perspective built on non-linear dynamics, multiple basins of attraction, thresholds, uncertainty and surprise, and cross-scale (fast/slow, small/large) interactions. A key move for our purposes is his explicit distinction between two things both called "resilience": a *narrow* engineering sense (return time to a single equilibrium) versus a *broad* ecological / social–ecological sense (how much disturbance a system can absorb while retaining its identity, plus its capacity to re-organize and adapt). He explicitly warns this is "not a paper for those that look for simple, clear-cut explanations about resilience in a technical sense" — the definitions are conceptual, not operational formulas.

## Key Concepts

The paper lays out a *sequence* of resilience concepts (its Table 1) running from narrow to broad. Defined exactly as the paper uses them:

- **Resilience (Holling 1973, the foundational definition).** "Resilience determines the persistence of relationships within a system and is a measure of the ability of these systems to absorb changes of state variables, driving variables, and parameters, and still persist" (Holling 1973, p. 17). The *useful measure* is "the size of stability domains, or, more meaningfully, the amount of disturbance a system can take before its controls shift to another set of variables and relationships that dominate another stability region." Focus is on variability and behaviour far from equilibrium, not on constancy.

- **Engineering resilience (the narrow interpretation).** Return time — "how fast a variable that has been displaced from equilibrium returns to it… the amount of time taken for the displacement to decay to some specified fraction of its initial value" (Pimm 1991). It applies only near a single stable equilibrium (linear or near-linear regime), and is about efficiency, constancy, and *resisting* change to conserve the existing state. In Table 1: characteristic = "return time, efficiency"; focus = "recovery, constancy"; context = "vicinity of a stable equilibrium."

- **Ecological / ecosystem resilience.** Buffer capacity: the ability to withstand shock and maintain function; focus on persistence and robustness; context = multiple equilibria / stability landscapes. This is the "how much disturbance before controls shift" reading, i.e. the Holling 1973 definition operating in a multi-basin world.

- **Social–ecological resilience (the broad, current definition).** "The capacity of a system to absorb disturbance and re-organize while undergoing change so as to still retain essentially the same function, structure, identity and feedbacks" (Walker et al. 2004). Following Carpenter et al. (2001) it has three components: (1) the amount of disturbance a system can absorb and still remain within the same state / domain of attraction; (2) the degree to which the system is capable of self-organization; (3) the degree to which the system can build and increase the capacity for learning and adaptation. Crucially, resilience here is *two-sided*: not only persistence/robustness ("sustaining") but also the renewal, recombination and emergence of new trajectories that disturbance opens up ("developing"). "Too much of either will ultimately lead to collapse." Resilience is not always good — a resilient system can be locked in an undesirable state and hard to transform.

- **Adaptability.** "The capacity of people in a social–ecological system to build resilience through collective action" (Walker et al. 2004). Folke ties resilience and adaptability tightly: "A vulnerable social–ecological system has lost resilience. Losing resilience implies loss of adaptability." Adaptability includes the capacity to respond to *and shape* ecosystem dynamics, not just cope socially.

- **Transformability.** "The capacity of people to create a fundamentally new social–ecological system when ecological, political, social, or economic conditions make the existing system untenable" (Walker et al. 2004). This is distinct from adaptability: adaptability keeps the current system viable; transformability abandons it for a new configuration.

- **Adaptive cycle (adaptive renewal cycle).** A *heuristic* model (not a law), generated from observed ecosystem dynamics, of four phases driven by discontinuous events: exploitation (**r**, exponential growth), conservation (**K**, growing stasis and rigidity), release / "creative destruction" (**Ω**, readjustment and collapse), and re-organization / renewal (**α**). A "front loop" of slow, gradual change (r→K) is followed by a "back loop" of rapid change (Ω→α) triggered by disturbance. Conventional management has focused on r and K and largely *ignored* the Ω and α back loop, yet these are "just as important." Disturbance is treated as part of development, not an anomaly. The **panarchy** extends this to a set of nested adaptive cycles across scales, coupled by cross-scale connections named "revolt" (a fast small-scale disturbance cascading up) and "remember" (slow large-scale memory — seed banks, surviving species, accumulated experience — seeding re-organization after disturbance).

- **Regime shifts / thresholds.** Because non-linearity produces path dependency and *multiple basins of attraction*, systems can cross a **threshold** and undergo a qualitative shift — a "flip" from one attractor / stability domain to another, where a different set of variables and relationships takes control. Folke prefers "regimes" or "attractors" over "stable states"/"equilibria," which wrongly imply the absence of dynamics. A regime shift's transition may be *sharp and dramatic* or *gradual*, but either way the system has moved to a different domain. He notes such shifts are increasingly human-caused via *erosion of resilience*, and that complex systems may "stutter" (show rising variance across scales) *in advance* of a regime shift — a possible early-warning indicator.

A note on vocabulary relevant to metric design: for the *broad* perspective Folke deliberately **avoids the word "recovery"** (an engineering-resilience term for return-to-previous-state), preferring **renewal, regeneration, re-organization**, because recovery trajectories are unique and often unpredictable and "the system may look similar but it is not the same system."

## Main Contribution

The paper's contribution is conceptual organization rather than new data: (1) it traces resilience from Holling's 1973 discovery of multiple basins of attraction through adaptive management to present-day SES research; (2) it sharply separates *engineering* resilience (return time, single equilibrium) from *ecological/social–ecological* resilience (absorptive capacity + self-organization + learning across multiple basins) — Table 1's ladder from narrow to broad is the single most reusable artefact; (3) it reframes resilience as two-sided (sustaining *and* developing), embedding it in complex-adaptive-systems theory, the adaptive cycle, and the panarchy; and (4) it positions adaptability and transformability as the human capacities that build or replace resilience. The takeaway for anyone building resilience metrics: *which* resilience you measure (return time vs. disturbance absorbed vs. capacity to re-organize) is a substantive modelling choice, and the engineering notion is a special case valid only near a single stable equilibrium.

## Limitations

- It is an explicitly narrative, non-technical review — Folke states it deliberately avoids "simple, clear-cut" or "technical sense" definitions. There are **no operational formulas**; the definitions are qualitative and must be operationalized by the reader.
- The definitions are drawn from and attributed to *other* authors (Holling 1973; Pimm 1991; Carpenter et al. 2001; Walker et al. 2004; Anderies et al. 2004) — this note is a secondary source for them; the primary sources should be consulted before committing a metric to a definition.
- The adaptive cycle and panarchy are, by Folke's own admission, *heuristics*; he notes critics find the adaptive cycle "too deterministic," and he openly self-identifies as an enthusiast rather than a neutral arbiter.
- Field evidence for multi-stable states was historically "only suggestive" (Holling's own words quoted); the concept was contested (Sousa and Connell 1985 found no evidence). Measuring thresholds empirically is acknowledged to be hard and to require very long, high-resolution time series set by the *slow* variable.
- Strongly ecology-derived; integrating the social dimension is described as "still in its infancy" / "explorative." The framework is largely for real-world SES governance, so mapping it onto a stylized simulation requires interpretation, not direct application.

## Relevance to This Project

Our simulation is a reproducible agent-based common-pool-resource (CPR) model; the planned **Phase 3** adds disturbances (resource shocks, agent failure) and measures resilience. Folke gives us the principled vocabulary to do this without hand-waving:

- **Pick the right resilience, and say so.** Folke's engineering-vs-ecological split maps directly onto two different metrics we could report. *Return time* to the pre-shock resource/cooperation level is **engineering resilience** — valid only if the system has a single stable regime and stays near it. The *magnitude of shock the system can absorb while staying in the cooperative/sustainable regime* is **ecological resilience** — the more meaningful measure in a model that (by design) has more than one basin. We should report both and label them as such, and not conflate "it came back fast" with "it can take a big hit."

- **Collapse to zero is a textbook threshold / regime shift, and an *absorbing* one.** In our model a resource driven to 0 cannot regenerate. In Folke's terms this is exactly the system's "controls shifting to another set of variables and relationships that dominate another stability region" — the depleted state is a second basin of attraction, and because it is absorbing it is a one-way threshold. This justifies treating resource extinction not as "very slow recovery" but as a *qualitatively different regime* that resilience metrics must flag categorically (survived vs. crossed the threshold), never as a continuous return-time number.

- **Prefer "re-organization" over "recovery" where the post-shock state differs.** Folke's warning that a recovered system "may look similar but it is not the same system" cautions us: if after a shock the resource level returns but the *strategy composition / cooperation regime* is different, we should measure that, not just the resource scalar. Post-shock *sustainability of cooperation* is the SES-resilience quantity, not mere resource rebound.

- **Rising variance as an early-warning signal.** Folke reports that complex systems "stutter" (increased variance across scales) *before* a regime shift. This is directly testable in our deterministic-plus-shock setting: we can log resource/harvest variance in the run-up to collapses and check whether variance rises before the threshold is crossed — a cheap, principled early-warning metric.

- **Adaptability vs. transformability distinction is available but likely out of Phase-3 scope.** Our agents currently do not "create a fundamentally new system," so *transformability* is not yet measurable; *adaptability* (building resilience through, e.g., reciprocity or sanctioning in response to a shock) is closer to what our conditional/sanctioning strategies could exhibit. Worth noting as a Phase-4+ direction rather than a Phase-3 metric.

## Important Terms

Candidates to add/clarify in `docs/terminology.md`, defined as Folke uses them:

- **Engineering resilience** — return time to a single equilibrium; speed of return after perturbation.
- **Ecological / ecosystem resilience** — amount of disturbance absorbed before the system shifts to another basin of attraction; buffer capacity, persistence, robustness.
- **Social–ecological resilience** — absorb + re-organize while retaining function, structure, identity, feedbacks (Walker et al. 2004); three components: disturbance absorbed, self-organization, capacity to learn/adapt (Carpenter et al. 2001).
- **Adaptability** — capacity to build resilience through collective action.
- **Transformability** — capacity to create a fundamentally new system when the current one is untenable.
- **Adaptive cycle (r, K, Ω, α)** — heuristic four-phase model; front loop (gradual) + back loop (rapid, disturbance-triggered).
- **Panarchy** — nested adaptive cycles across scales, coupled by "revolt" and "remember."
- **Regime shift / threshold** — flip between basins of attraction; can be sharp or gradual; controls shift to a different set of variables.
- **Basin / domain of attraction, stability landscape** — the region within which the system persists; its size is the intuitive measure of resilience.
- **Erosion of resilience** — human-driven loss of buffer capacity that makes previously absorbable disturbances able to trigger a shift.
- **Rising variance / "stutter"** — increased variance ahead of a regime shift; possible early-warning indicator.

## Questions

- The operational definitions we most need (return time, absorbed-disturbance magnitude, three-component resilience) are *cited* here, not derived. Should we read the primaries — Carpenter et al. (2001) "From metaphor to measurement: resilience of what to what?", Walker et al. (2004), and Anderies et al. (2004, robustness) — before fixing our metric definitions? (Carpenter's title alone suggests it is the metric-focused paper we actually want.)
- "Resilience of *what* to *what*" (Carpenter et al. 2001): for our model we must state both explicitly — resilience *of the cooperative/sustainable regime* to *resource shocks and agent failure*. Do we also want resilience of *cooperation* to *invasion by defectors*, or is that a separate (stability) question?
- Does our model actually have more than one non-trivial basin, or only "sustained" vs. "collapsed-to-zero"? If the latter, ecological resilience collapses to a survival threshold and return-time metrics only make sense within the surviving regime — worth confirming before Phase 3 design.
- Is the "rising variance before collapse" signal detectable at all in a (near-)deterministic model, or does it require the stochastic shock process to be visible? Needs a small pilot.
