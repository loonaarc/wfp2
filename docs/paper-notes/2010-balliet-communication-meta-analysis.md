# Communication and Cooperation in Social Dilemmas: A Meta-Analytic Review

Read status: 🟢 read from the PDF.

## Citation
Balliet, D. (2010). Communication and cooperation in social dilemmas: A
meta-analytic review. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*, 54(1), 39–57.
https://doi.org/10.1177/0022002709352443
(Published online first 3 December 2009; version of record 15 January 2010.
Author affiliation at time of writing: Singapore Management University. PDF read
is the OnlineFirst layout, paginated 1–19.)

## Research Problem
Communication is the single most-studied and most-effective remedy for social
dilemmas — the well-established "communication enhances cooperation" finding goes
back to Deutsch (1958). But the prior quantitative synthesis (Sally 1995) reported
only an aggregate magnitude ("communication increases cooperation by ~40%") and ran
**no moderator analyses**. So the field knew *that* communication helps, but not
*how much* on a standardized scale, nor *under which conditions* it helps more or
less. Balliet's paper sets out to (a) re-estimate the overall communication →
cooperation effect as a standardized effect size across the broader modern
literature, and (b) test three study-design moderators: communication medium
(face-to-face talk vs. written messages), timing (communication before vs. also
during iterated play), and group size.

## Method (meta-analysis)
- **Design:** Hedges & Olkin (1985) fixed/mixed-effects meta-analysis; computed in
  Comprehensive Meta-Analysis v2. A **mixed-effects model** was chosen (systematic +
  random variation assumed); discrepancies with a fixed-effects model were to be
  reported. Effect size = **standardized mean difference *d*** in cooperation between
  a communication condition and a no-communication control. Positive *d* = more
  cooperation with communication. Overall heterogeneity was significant,
  *Q*(44) = 282.9, *p* < .001, justifying the mixed model.
- **Number of studies / effect sizes:** **45 eligible effect sizes** (the literature
  search spanned databases — PsycINFO, EconLit — cited reviews, unpublished
  manuscripts, and solicitation of attendees of the 12th International Conference for
  Social Dilemmas). Sample sizes of individual studies range from ~36 to 512
  participants (see the paper's Table 2).
- **Inclusion criteria:** (1) must *manipulate* (not merely measure) communication;
  (2) must have a proper no-communication control condition; (3) outcome must be a
  genuine social dilemma (PD, public-goods/give-some, resource/take-some). Dictator,
  bargaining, and trust games were **excluded**.
- **Moderators coded and tested:**
  - **Communication medium:** written message (coded 1, *n* = 12) vs. discussion/talk
    (coded 2, *n* = 32). One study mixed both and was dropped from that moderator test.
  - **Timing (before vs. during):** communication only before the first trial
    (coded 1, *n* = 17) vs. ongoing communication during the dilemma (coded 2,
    *n* = 28). Tested **only within iterated games**.
  - **Group size:** continuous, ranging **2 to 9 persons** (mode = 2, *n* = 13 / ~30%
    of studies); entered as the predictor in a **meta-regression**.
- **Robustness:** Orwin's fail-safe *N* = 286 (threshold for confidence 230) — robust
  to publication bias. Two extreme outliers (Frohlich & Oppenheimer 1998, *d* = 8.37;
  Kinukawa, Saijo & Une 2000, *d* = 12.09 — both small-*n* studies where the
  communication condition produced near-unanimous cooperation) were flagged, and
  **every analysis is reported both with and without them.**

## Main Results
**Headline effect — communication has a large positive effect on cooperation:**
**_d_ = 1.01**, 95% CI [0.82, 1.20] (all 45 effect sizes). Removing the two outliers
lowers it only slightly to **_d_ = 0.92**, 95% CI [0.76, 1.07]. The overall effect
can equivalently be expressed as a correlation **_r_ = .49**, 95% CI [.40, .57]
(paper's Note 1). By Cohen's conventions *d* ≈ 1.0 is a "large" effect.

Moderators:
- **Medium (face-to-face talk > written) — significant.** Talk **_d_ = 1.21**,
  95% CI [0.98, 1.44] vs. written messages **_d_ = 0.46**, 95% CI [0.25, 0.67];
  *Q*(1) = 22.35, *p* < .001. Without outliers: talk **_d_ = 1.07** [0.89, 1.30]
  vs. written **_d_ = 0.46** [0.25, 0.67], *Q*(1) = 18.56, *p* < .001. So written /
  computer-mediated communication still helps, but roughly **half to a third** as
  strongly as face-to-face discussion.
- **Group size — larger groups → stronger effect (significant/marginal).**
  Meta-regression slope = **.033**, *Z* = 1.96, *p* = .05 with outliers; slope = .032,
  *Z* = 1.87, *p* = .06 without. As group size grows, communication becomes *more*
  effective (relative to no-communication) — interpreted as communication buffering
  the usual decline of cooperation in larger groups.
- **Timing (before vs. during) — no significant difference.** Within iterated games:
  during-dilemma **_d_ = 1.13** [0.83, 1.43] vs. before-only **_d_ = 0.87** [0.67,
  1.08], *Q*(1) = 1.92, *p* = .17. Without outliers: during **_d_ = 0.95** [0.72,
  1.19] vs. before **_d_ = 0.87** [0.67, 1.08], *Q*(1) = 0.24, *p* = .63. **Pregame
  ("one-shot") communication is about as effective as continuous communication** —
  the effect of a pre-play discussion persists after communication is removed.

## Limitations
- **Publication bias / outliers.** Almost all included studies are published; two
  extreme outliers (small samples with near-unanimous cooperation) can inflate the
  estimate — hence the with/without reporting. Fail-safe *N* mitigates but does not
  eliminate this.
- **Weak test of group size.** Range only 2–9, mode = 2 (~30% are 2-person). Too few
  large-group studies to strongly test the group-size interaction.
- **Weak test of timing.** Mode of iterations = 10; the persistence-of-communication
  effect might only emerge with many more iterations; the ≥10-iteration subset test
  is underpowered (Type II risk).
- **Untested moderators.** Not enough studies to test **type of dilemma**, index of
  conflict (Rapoport's *k*), or proportion of prosocials. So the meta-analysis cannot
  say whether communication helps *more* in higher-conflict dilemmas or in
  resource/commons vs. PD settings — a gap directly relevant to us.
- **Manipulated-only.** Studies that merely measured (correlated) communication were
  excluded, so results speak to *designed* communication opportunities.
- **Cooperation, not sustainability.** The outcome is a cooperation index (contribution
  / cooperative choice), *not* long-run resource survival. The meta-analysis never
  distinguishes "cooperation went up" from "the resource was saved."

## Relevance to This Project
This paper is the empirical yardstick for our **E6 communication** experiment.
- **Calibrates the size of the human effect.** Across 45 human studies, communication
  moves cooperation by a *large* amount (**_d_ ≈ 1.0**, _r_ ≈ .49). Our E6 result
  (broadcast reliability drives payoff Gini from 0.52 down toward the
  global-information 0.14) is a *large* effect in the same direction, so E6 is **not
  overstating** the human communication effect — if anything, the human literature
  says communication is powerful, consistent with our fairness gain being big.
- **Sharpens the E6 headline caveat.** Balliet measures *cooperation*, never *resource
  survival*. Our E6 finding — communication improves **fairness monotonically but
  sustainability stays 0.00** — is therefore not contradicted by Balliet; it lives in
  a distinction his outcome measure cannot make. This is a genuine contribution angle:
  the meta-analytic "communication helps cooperation" masks whether the *resource*
  survives, which depends (per E6/E7) on the **response rule**, not on communication
  per se. We should state this explicitly: *"communication raises cooperation (d≈1.0
  in humans) but that says nothing about whether the common pool survives."*
- **Medium moderator ↔ our reliability sweep.** Balliet's strongest moderator is
  medium: face-to-face (*d* = 1.21) ≫ written (*d* = 0.46). Our `broadcast_reliability`
  sweep is a *degradation* axis conceptually analogous to moving from rich
  (face-to-face) to lossy (written/computer-mediated) channels — and we likewise find
  a **graded, monotonic** weakening as the channel degrades. Good qualitative match;
  worth citing as external validation that channel quality matters continuously.
- **Two concrete moderators we could add.** (1) **Group size** — Balliet finds the
  effect *grows* with group size (slope ≈ .033); our E6 fixes one population (6 CC + 2
  selfish). Sweeping group size / free-rider ratio would test whether our model
  reproduces the human "communication helps more in larger groups" trend. (2)
  **Timing / repetition** — Balliet finds pregame ≈ continuous (persistence). Our
  broadcast is every-round; a cheap test is a *pregame-only* or intermittent broadcast
  to see whether our conditional cooperators sustain the fairness gain after the
  channel goes silent (they likely will *not*, since our mechanism is per-round
  detection, not an internalized norm — an informative divergence from humans).
- **Mechanism gap to flag.** Balliet attributes the human effect largely to
  **norms (promise-keeping) and group identity**, i.e. an *internalized* commitment
  that persists without further communication. Our agents have no such internal norm —
  they react to the current signal. That difference predicts our timing result should
  *differ* from Balliet's null (pregame should be weaker for us), which is a testable,
  publishable contrast.

## Important Terms
- **Standardized mean difference (Cohen's _d_):** effect size = (mean difference in
  cooperation) / pooled SD; *d* ≈ 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8+ large.
- **Mixed-effects meta-analysis:** assumes both systematic (moderator) and random
  between-study variation; contrast with fixed-effects (population = sample) and
  random-effects (only random variation).
- **Heterogeneity _Q_ statistic:** tests whether effect sizes vary more than sampling
  error alone would predict.
- **Orwin's fail-safe _N_:** number of null-result studies needed to drag the pooled
  effect down to triviality; a publication-bias robustness check.
- **Cheap talk:** non-binding, costless pre-play communication (the game-theory term
  for what these experiments manipulate).
- **Give-some (public-goods) vs. take-some (resource/commons) dilemma:** the two CPR-
  relevant dilemma families in the sample; PD is the third and most common.
- **Promise-keeping norm / group identity:** the two leading explanations for *why*
  communication works in this literature.

## Questions
- Does the group-size effect hold if re-estimated on give-some/take-some (CPR-type)
  studies only? Balliet couldn't test dilemma-type as a moderator — our sim could
  probe whether the group-size trend is CPR-specific.
- Our E6 response is *retaliation*; Balliet's human effect is attributed to
  *norm-based restraint*. If we swap in a restraint/pledge responder (the E6/E7
  follow-up), do we recover both the fairness *and* a resource benefit — i.e. move
  closer to the human mechanism? (Ties directly to Janssen et al. 2022:
  communication → trust → restraint.)
- What is the *sustainability* analogue of Balliet's *d*? There is none in the human
  literature reviewed — is "communication raises cooperation but not resource
  survival" itself a gap our reproducible sim is well-placed to fill?
