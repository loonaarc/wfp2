## Citation
Ostrom, E. (1990). *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for
Collective Action*. Cambridge University Press. (Canto Classics reissue 2015;
ISBN 978-1-107-56978-2.) Part of the series "The Political Economy of Institutions
and Decisions." ~280 pp.

## Research Problem
Natural resources used in common by many appropriators — inshore fisheries,
mountain meadows and forests, irrigation systems, groundwater basins — are widely
predicted to be over-exploited and destroyed. Yet Ostrom observes that "communities
of individuals have relied on institutions resembling neither the state nor the
market to govern some resource systems with reasonable degrees of success over long
periods of time" (p. 1). The book asks: *under what institutional conditions do
groups of appropriators succeed (or fail) at governing a common-pool resource (CPR)
by themselves*, without either privatization or an external Leviathan? She deliberately
restricts scope to small-scale, single-country CPRs with roughly 50–15,000 users who
are heavily economically dependent on the resource; renewable rather than
non-renewable; scarce rather than abundant; and where users can harm one another but
not impose major external harm (asymmetric pollution and cartels are excluded)
(pp. 26–27).

## Why the Problem Is Difficult
Three "influential models" (ch. 1) — Hardin's *tragedy of the commons* (1968), the
*prisoner's dilemma* game, and Olson's *logic of collective action* (1965) — all share
a common core: the **free-rider problem**. Whoever cannot be excluded from a collective
benefit is individually motivated not to contribute, so rational actors produce an
outcome none of them wants (pp. 2–7). Beyond first-order free-riding, Ostrom identifies
(from ch. 2) three deeper puzzles that any self-governance solution must solve:
- **Supply** of new institutions (who provides the rules, itself a public good);
- **Credible commitment** (why keep a rule *ex post* when temptations arise, given that
  "agreeing to follow rules *ex ante* is an easy commitment to make" (p. 93));
- **Mutual monitoring** — the hardest. Monitoring and sanctioning are themselves costly
  public goods, so nobody should want to provide them. This is the **second-order
  dilemma**: as Elster puts it, "punishment almost invariably is costly to the punisher,
  while the benefits from punishment are diffusely distributed over the members" (quoted
  p. 95).

## Core Argument
Ostrom's central move is to attack the *metaphorical use of models*. The three models
assume constraints are fixed; but "the prisoners in the famous dilemma cannot change
the constraints imposed on them ... Not all users of natural resources are similarly
incapable of changing their constraints" (p. 7). Policy built on the metaphor collapses
into two "only way" prescriptions — central regulation ("Leviathan") or privatization —
both of which assume institutional change must be *imposed from outside* (pp. 8–13).

She undermines both with a sequence of games (pp. 8–17). The central-authority solution
(Game 2) works only under complete information and costless, error-free sanctioning;
with *incomplete* information (Games 3–4) the regulator makes sanctioning errors, and
the regulated game can end up **worse** than the unregulated one — at a correct-sanction
probability of 0.7 the equilibrium payoff is (−1.4, −1.4) versus (0, 0) with no
regulation; the agency needs to sanction correctly with probability > 0.75 merely to
avoid making things worse (p. 11). Against this she poses Game 5, a **self-financed
contract-enforcement game**: the appropriators themselves negotiate a binding contract,
share the enforcement cost *e*, and arrange an enforcer — and crucially "the participants
themselves design their own contracts ... in light of the information they have at hand"
and "have an incentive to report contractual infractions" as a by-product (p. 17). The
empirical anchor is the **Alanya, Turkey** inshore fishery (pp. 18–20), where fishers
devised a rotating lottery for fishing sites that is monitored and enforced by the
fishers themselves. Her thesis: self-governance is a real, empirically attested third
option, and the task of theory is to identify the conditions under which it works.

## The Eight Design Principles
From ch. 3, Ostrom distills what she calls a set of **seven** design principles shared by
all the long-enduring cases, "plus an eighth principle used in the larger, more complex
cases" (p. 89). She is explicit that these are "still quite speculative" and that she is
"not yet willing to argue that these design principles are necessary conditions" (p. 90).
The definitions below are quoted verbatim from Table 3.1 (p. 90):

1. **Clearly defined boundaries** — "Individuals or households who have rights to
   withdraw resource units from the CPR must be clearly defined, as must the boundaries
   of the CPR itself."
2. **Congruence between appropriation and provision rules and local conditions** —
   "Appropriation rules restricting time, place, technology, and/or quantity of resource
   units are related to local conditions and to provision rules requiring labor,
   material, and/or money."
3. **Collective-choice arrangements** — "Most individuals affected by the operational
   rules can participate in modifying the operational rules."
4. **Monitoring** — "Monitors, who actively audit CPR conditions and appropriator
   behavior, are accountable to the appropriators or are the appropriators."
5. **Graduated sanctions** — "Appropriators who violate operational rules are likely to
   be assessed graduated sanctions (depending on the seriousness and context of the
   offense) by other appropriators, by officials accountable to these appropriators, or
   by both."
6. **Conflict-resolution mechanisms** — "Appropriators and their officials have rapid
   access to low-cost local arenas to resolve conflicts among appropriators or between
   appropriators and officials."
7. **Minimal recognition of rights to organize** — "The rights of appropriators to devise
   their own institutions are not challenged by external governmental authorities."
8. **Nested enterprises** (for CPRs that are parts of larger systems) — "Appropriation,
   provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict resolution, and governance activities are
   organized in multiple layers of nested enterprises."

## How the Principles Were Derived
The principles are the *common similarities* distilled from a comparative case study of
CPR institutions selected precisely because they endured — the youngest is over 100 years
old, the oldest over 1,000 years, and all "have survived droughts, floods, wars,
pestilence, and major economic and political changes" (p. 58). The cases (ch. 3):
- **Communal tenure in high mountain meadows and forests**: Torbel, Switzerland
  (documented by Netting; e.g. the 1517 "wintering rule" — no citizen may send more cows
  to the alp than he can feed over winter — enforced with fines by a local official who
  keeps half the fine, pp. 61–62); and the Japanese villages of Hirano, Nagaike, and
  Yamanaka (McKean).
- **Huerta irrigation institutions** in eastern Spain: Valencia, Murcia, Orihuela, and
  Alicante — four nearby systems whose *rules differ substantially* to fit differing
  water storage and scarcity (pp. 69–81).
- **Zanjera irrigation communities** in the Philippines (Siy) — included specifically to
  test whether the principles derived from developed-country cases also hold in a Third
  World setting; Ostrom answers yes (pp. 82–88).

Method: the *operational rules differ markedly* across cases, so the rules themselves
cannot explain endurance; instead Ostrom looks one level up, for shared **design
principles**. A "design principle" is "an essential element or condition that helps to
account for the success of these institutions in sustaining the CPRs and gaining the
compliance of generation after generation" (p. 90). The cases are explicitly *not a
random sample* (p. 26); they were chosen for clear information and for institutional
"robustness" in Shepsle's sense. Ch. 5 (skimmed) tests the principles against *failed*
CPRs.

## Main Results
- All the robust institutions exhibit principles 1–7 (and the complex ones also 8),
  despite wildly different operational rules — supporting the claim that endurance is
  explained at the level of design principles, not specific rules.
- **Monitoring and sanctioning are done by the appropriators themselves, not external
  authorities**, and the initial sanctions are "surprisingly low" / graduated (p. 94).
- The **second-order dilemma is overcome** because the rules make monitoring a *low-cost
  by-product* of ordinary use: rotation systems put "the two actors most concerned with
  cheating in direct contact with one another," so "monitoring is a by-product of their
  own strong motivations" (p. 95). Monitoring also yields *private* benefits — status and
  prestige for catching an infractor, a share of the fines (Spanish guards keep part of
  the fine; Japanese "detectives" keep the confiscated sake, p. 96), and information
  needed to sustain a *contingent strategy* ("quasi-voluntary compliance," after Levi:
  people comply willingly only while confident others comply and that enforcement means
  they won't be played for a "sucker," pp. 94–95).
- **Graduated** sanctions beat large first-offense fines: a heavy fine on someone facing
  an unusual hardship "may produce resentment and unwillingness to conform in the future"
  (p. 98). Real compliance need not be 100% — tolerance rises in genuine emergencies
  (McKean's 1930s-depression Yamanaka example, p. 98) but tightens toward 100% when a
  single infraction can do great harm (the Shiwa drought, all adult males patrolling the
  dikes nightly, p. 99).
- Commitment and monitoring are *strategically linked*: contingent self-commitment
  ("I commit to follow the rules ... if the rest of those affected make a similar
  commitment and act accordingly", pp. 99–100) and mutual monitoring reinforce each other.

## Limitations
Stated by Ostrom herself:
- The design principles are "still quite speculative"; she does **not** claim they are
  necessary conditions — "further theoretical and empirical work is needed before a
  strong assertion of necessity can be made" (p. 90).
- Scope is deliberately narrow (small-scale, renewable, scarce, single-country,
  50–15,000 dependent users, no major external harm; pp. 26–27), so generalization to
  global commons, pollution, or large-N settings is not claimed.
- Cases are selected on endurance/success, not sampled randomly (survivorship is
  acknowledged; ch. 5 partly offsets this with failure cases).
- The account is about *incentive compatibility and correlates of robustness*, not a
  formally proven causal mechanism; optimality is explicitly disclaimed ("I do not claim
  that the institutions devised in these settings are in any sense 'optimal'", p. 59).
- Later literature (e.g. Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás 2010) empirically revisits and
  sub-divides the principles — not read here, flagged for follow-up.

## Relevance to This Project
This is the load-bearing institutional reference for the project's monitoring/sanctioning
direction. Mapping Ostrom onto our experiments:
- **E3 (monitoring + harvest-quota sanction protects resource and fairness but creates a
  second-order free-rider problem).** This is *exactly* Ostrom's principles 4–5 and her
  second-order dilemma. But two mismatches matter. (a) Our sanction is a flat, binding
  harvest cap, **not graduated** by "seriousness and context" (principle 5). (b) Our
  monitoring is not made cheap or rewarding: the enduring commons overcame the
  second-order dilemma because rules turned monitoring into a *low-cost by-product* with
  private payoffs (status, fine-shares, strategic information), a structural feature our
  model does not implement. So E3's second-order free-riding is the *expected* outcome of
  a model that strips out precisely the mechanisms Ostrom says make monitoring
  self-sustaining.
- **E5 (voluntary monitoring is not evolutionarily stable).** This reproduces the classic
  Elster prediction (punishment costly, benefits diffuse) — which is the *default* Ostrom
  says you get *without* the surrounding design principles. Her empirical answer is that
  real institutions *engineer* monitoring to be cheap and personally rewarding; our model
  lacks that engineering, so instability is unsurprising rather than a general truth.
- **E7 (only enforcement — a binding constraint — saves the commons; peer responses to
  communication do not).** Consistent with Ostrom/Levi that *coercion is an essential
  condition* for quasi-voluntary compliance (Game 5's binding contract needs an enforcer).
  But Ostrom's deeper point recontextualises the result: in the field, enforcement is
  *internal and self-financed* and, critically, *coupled to collective choice* (principle
  3) — communication matters because it lets appropriators **design** the rules and the
  enforcement, not because talk alone shifts behavior. Our model's "communication" is cheap
  talk decoupled from rule-design and self-organized enforcement, so its failure in E7
  reflects that impoverished coupling, not a verdict that communication is useless in
  commons governance.

**What the simulation currently represents:** monitoring (principle 4, crudely), a
sanction (principle 5, but flat not graduated), and a binding enforcement constraint (the
spirit of Game 5).
**What it cannot yet represent:** clearly defined boundaries / who may harvest (1),
congruence and local rule-tailoring (2), agents modifying their own rules — collective
choice (3), conflict-resolution (6), rights to organize (7), and nested enterprises (8);
plus *accountable* monitoring and *graduated* sanctions, and low-cost by-product
monitoring.

**Crucial caveat for our write-up:** our "any one monitor enforces fully" simplification
is **cruder than, and in tension with, Ostrom's principle 4**. Principle 4 stresses that
monitors are *accountable to the appropriators or are the appropriators*; our all-or-
nothing external-style enforcer is closer to the Leviathan Ostrom argues *against*, and
it also ignores the redundancy and accountability ("guards are monitored too", p. 96)
that keep real monitoring honest.

## Important Terms
- **Common-pool resource (CPR)** — a resource system where exclusion is costly and one
  user's appropriation subtracts from what is available to others (defined precisely in
  ch. 2; renewable, scarce cases only here).
- **Appropriator / provision** — those who withdraw resource units; provision = supplying
  or maintaining the resource/infrastructure (labor, materials, money).
- **Design principle** — an essential condition accounting for the success and durability
  of a CPR institution (p. 90).
- **Second-order dilemma** — the collective-action problem of providing monitoring and
  sanctioning, which are themselves costly public goods.
- **Quasi-voluntary compliance** (Levi) — compliance that is voluntary but backed by the
  credible coercion of non-compliers; contingent on believing others comply.
- **Contingent self-commitment** — "I will follow the rules so long as most others do."
- **Institutional robustness** (Shepsle) — rules changing over time according to the
  institution's own collective-/constitutional-choice rules, i.e. "in equilibrium."
- **Metaphorical use of models** — treating a model's fixed constraints as if they were
  fixed in the real world, thereby smuggling in "only way" policy conclusions.

## Questions
- Which design principles are *representable* in our abstract model, and which require
  richer structure (boundaries, collective choice, nesting, conflict resolution)?
- Can we replace E3's flat quota with a **graduated** sanction (severity scaled to
  repeat-offense/context) and re-test whether it eases the second-order free-rider
  problem?
- Can monitoring be re-modelled as a *low-cost by-product* with private payoffs (status,
  fine-share, strategic information), per Ostrom's explanation, to see whether voluntary
  monitoring becomes stable in E5?
- Is "any one monitor enforces fully" defensible against principle 4's emphasis on
  *accountable* monitoring and redundancy? What breaks if we make monitors themselves
  monitorable and firable?
- E7 says communication without a binding constraint fails — could a **collective-choice**
  channel (agents agreeing on and adjusting the rule, not just signaling) succeed where
  cheap talk does not?
