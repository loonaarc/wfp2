# Altruistic Punishment in Humans

Read status: 🟡 noted from the abstract and widely-cited secondary summaries (the
*Nature* full text is paywalled). Verify specific numbers and the experimental design

## Citation
Fehr, E., & Gächter, S. (2002). Altruistic punishment in humans. *Nature*, 415(6868),
137–140. https://doi.org/10.1038/415137a

## Research Problem
Why do humans cooperate in one-shot / non-repeated public-good situations where
narrow self-interest predicts defection? The paper tests whether the *option to
punish* defectors — even at a cost to the punisher and with no material return —
changes cooperation.

## Why the Problem Is Difficult
Punishing a defector is itself a *costly, non-rewarded* act: a purely self-interested
individual should never pay to punish (and should free-ride on others' punishment).
So sustaining cooperation via punishment is a puzzle at two levels — cooperation
itself, and the willingness to punish.

## Proposed Method
Public-goods economics experiments with human subjects, comparing a treatment where
players can pay to punish other players after seeing contributions against a control
with no punishment option, across partner/stranger matchings.

## Metrics
Contribution levels to the public good over rounds; frequency and targeting of
punishment; the relationship between under-contribution and punishment received.

## Main Results
- **Cooperation flourishes when punishment is available and breaks down when it is
  not.** Contributions rise (often toward full cooperation) with punishment and decay
  toward free-riding without it.
- Punishment is **targeted at defectors** and is administered even when it yields the
  punisher no material benefit ("altruistic punishment").
- The proximate driver is **negative emotion toward free-riders**.

## Limitations
Lab setting with monetary stakes; punishment is frictionless and accurately targeted;
does not by itself resolve *who bears the punishment cost* in equilibrium (the
second-order problem — see Hauert et al. 2007).

## Future Work
Evolutionary/mechanistic accounts of how costly punishing dispositions are sustained;
institutional vs. peer punishment.

## Relevance to This Project
This is the empirical backbone for **Experiment E3**: enforcement/punishment sustains
cooperation where unstructured cooperation fails. Our sanctioning result reproduces
the *direction* of this finding in an abstract CPR model. Two differences worth
noting:
- Their punishment reduces a defector's *payoff* (deterrence works because humans
  *adapt*); our fixed selfish agents don't adapt, so our sanctioning instead caps
  *extraction* directly (ADR-0005) to get the same protective effect.
- Their "altruistic" framing foregrounds that punishing is costly and unrewarded —
  exactly our **second-order free-rider** result (monitors earn less).

## Possible Follow-Up Contribution
An *adaptive* selfish agent that reduces greed when punished would let us reproduce
Fehr & Gächter-style deterrence (rather than confiscation) and compare the two
enforcement designs. It also sets up voluntary/optional punishment (Hauert et al.).
