# Covenants With and Without a Sword: Self-Governance Is Possible

Read status: 🟢 read from the PDF.

## Citation
Ostrom, E., Walker, J., & Gardner, R. (1992). "Covenants With and Without a Sword:
Self-Governance Is Possible." *American Political Science Review*, 86(2), 404–417.
(Indiana University, Bloomington. NSF grant SES-8843901.) Verified against the PDF's
running head and page numbers (Vol. 86, No. 2, June 1992, pp. 404–417).

## Research Problem
Contemporary political theory (Hobbes) and non-cooperative game theory (Nash;
Harsanyi & Selten) both assume that individuals **cannot make credible ex ante
commitments** where ex post temptations to break them exist, *unless* those
commitments are enforced by an **external agent** — the "sword" of a Leviathan.
Applied to **common-pool resources (CPRs)** — resources whose yield is *subtractable*
and whose *exclusion is nontrivial* (fisheries, forests, grazing ranges, irrigation,
groundwater) — this assumption yields the policy conclusion that only coercive
external government (or privatization) can avert the tragedy of the commons (Ophuls
1973). Yet field evidence shows appropriators repeatedly self-organize, monitor each
other, and sanction rule-breakers *without* external authorities. The paper resolves
three standing anomalies by experimentally isolating the independent and interactive
effects of (1) **communication (covenant) alone**, one-shot and repeated; (2)
**sanctioning (sword) alone**; and (3) **communication combined with an internal
sword**. The theoretical benchmark is sharp: because talk has no payoff consequence,
the subgame-consistent equilibrium is *identical* across all treatments (each subject
invests the Nash amount), so under standard theory none of these treatments should
change behavior.

## Experimental Setup (the CPR lab game)
An **n-person CPR appropriation game** run on the NOVANET computer system with Indiana
University undergraduates who all had **prior experience** in a CPR environment.
- **n = 8** appropriators per group. Each has a token endowment split between **Market 1**
  (safe outside option, constant return **5¢/unit**) and **Market 2** (the CPR, return
  **1¢/unit of output**, output shared in proportion to one's share of total group
  investment). Quadratic production function **F(Σxᵢ) = 23(Σxᵢ) − 0.25(Σxᵢ)²**.
- Two **endowment treatments** to vary disequilibrium stakes: **low = 10 tokens/subject**,
  **high = 25 tokens/subject**. The Nash equilibrium (each invests **8 tokens**, total
  64) and the group optimum (total **36 tokens**, i.e. 4–5 each) are *identical* across
  endowments; only the room to over-invest differs. With 25 tokens, as few as **3**
  subjects dumping everything can drive CPR returns below the safe rate; with 10 tokens
  it takes **7 of 8** — so the high-endowment game is the harder, more disequilibrating
  environment.
- **Information:** subjects knew group size, total tokens, equal endowments, and the
  production schedule (functional + tabular), but **individual decisions were anonymous**;
  in the baseline they saw only *aggregate* Market-2 investment each round and did **not**
  know the exact number of rounds. Baseline = 20 rounds of the constituent game.

**Treatments** (see the paper's Figure 1). Communication periods, when present, occurred
**after round 10** (baseline for rounds 1–10, treatment thereafter), except two
"at-the-outset" checks:
- **Covenant without a sword — communication alone.**
  - *One-shot:* a single **10-minute** face-to-face discussion after round 10, then the
    game resumes. Restrictions: no side payments, no physical threats, no viewing others'
    monitors. Talk is non-binding and has no payoff consequence.
  - *Repeated:* discussion after **every** subsequent round (subjects sit facing one
    another). Run in both low- and high-endowment settings.
- **Sword without a covenant — sanctioning alone.** After round 10, each subject may pay
  a **fee** to levy a **fine** on another (identified only by subject number; the fined
  party does not learn who fined them). Real resources are consumed, not redistributed.
  Fees ranged **5¢–40¢**, fines **10¢–80¢**, **fee-to-fine ratio 0.25 or 0.50**. No
  discussion of *why* or *how* to use it was permitted — kept as close to the
  non-cooperative benchmark as possible.
- **Covenants with a sword — communication + sanctioning.** Two designs: (a) **imposed**
  one-shot communication *plus* an imposed sanctioning mechanism; and (b) **endogenous** —
  one-shot communication followed by a **majority vote** on whether to adopt a sanctioning
  mechanism and at what fine level (status quo = no mechanism). A hysteresis check ran the
  communication+choice option from the very outset.

## Metrics
- **Average net yield from the CPR as a percentage of maximum possible net yield**
  (Market-2 return minus opportunity cost of tokens, normalized so 100% = group optimum,
  0% = the point where CPR investment is twice optimal). Reported per round-block
  (Table 2) and aggregated per design (Table 3). Note (Table 3): the **Nash equilibrium ≈
  39% net CPR yield** for all designs — the theoretical benchmark all treatments are
  measured against.
- **Net yield after subtracting fees and fines** — the efficiency-relevant figure for
  sanctioning designs (sanctioning burns real resources).
- **Defection rate** — fraction of decisions where a subject invested *more* in the CPR
  than the group agreement specified.
- Qualitative coding of **sanctioning targets** (who gets fined and why) and analysis of
  discussion **transcripts**.

## Main Results
Aggregate net CPR yields (Table 3; "net of fees/fines" and defection where reported):

| Design (endowment) | Net yield | Net of fees/fines | Defection |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Baseline (10TK / 25TK) | 34% / 21% | — | — |
| One-shot communication (25TK) | 55% | — | 25% |
| Repeated communication (10TK / 25TK) | 99% / 73% | — | 5% / 13% |
| Sanction alone (25TK) | 37% | **9%** | — |
| One-shot comm. + imposed sanction (25TK) | 85% | 67% | 1% |
| One-shot comm. + choice — **sanction chosen** | 93% | 90% | 4% |
| One-shot comm. + choice — **no sanction chosen** | 56% | — | 42% |

- **Baseline (state of nature).** Individuals acting alone do **not** solve the dilemma.
  Yields track the Nash prediction at the aggregate level (last five baseline rounds ≈
  36% low / 32% high vs. ~38% predicted), though no single experiment ever *stabilized*
  at Nash — investments showed a "pulsing" over-invest/retreat pattern, dipping far below
  zero in the high-endowment case.
- **Communication alone helps — substantially, and against theory.** One-shot
  communication lifted the high-endowment game from a pre-talk **−27%** (first 10 rounds)
  to **74%** in the five rounds *immediately after* discussion, then decayed to a
  post-communication average of **55%** (defection **25%**). Repeated communication did
  far better and *sustained* it: **99%** of optimum in the low-endowment game (defection
  **5%**) but only **73%** in the harder high-endowment game (defection **13%**) — verbal
  sanctions ("Some scum-bucket is investing more than we agreed upon") were often enough
  to correct anonymous defectors, but were less effective when a few big investors could
  disequilibrate the pool. Headline comparison (Conclusions): low-endowment yield rose
  **35% → 99%** (no comm → repeated comm); high-endowment **21% → 55% → 73%** (none →
  one-shot → repeated). No group ever found the exact optimum, and one-shot agreements
  **decayed** over time.
- **Sanctioning alone helps gross yield but is inefficient — and overused.** Gross net
  yield rose **−38% → 37%** with the sword, but **net of fees and fines only 9%**:
  sanctioning without communication is not an efficient institution. Subjects sanctioned
  **far more than predicted** (subgame consistency predicts *zero*): **176** sanctioning
  instances across 8 experiments, never fewer than 10 in any one; frequency **inversely
  related to fee cost** and rising with fine size. Targeting was mostly rational (**77%**
  of fines hit the previous round's above-average investors, **+7%** earlier heavy
  investors) but a meaningful share was **not**: **5% "blind revenge"**, **11% error**.
- **Covenants WITH a sword is best.** Imposed communication + imposed sanction reached
  **85%** (67% net of costs, defection **1%**). When subjects could **choose** the sword
  by majority vote, only **2 of 4** groups adopted it — but adopters reached **93%** yield
  (**90%** net of costs, defection **4%**), approaching the conditions of a *cooperative*
  game, while non-adopters languished at **56%** (defection **42%**). Two
  communication+choice-from-outset groups averaged **95%** (94% net). Prior bad experience
  with a punitive/inefficient 20¢/80¢ sanctioning regime made subjects **vote against**
  adopting a sword (11 of 14 "no" votes came from that design), showing institutional
  history matters.
- **No trigger strategies.** Contrary to folk-theorem intuition, in **no** experiment did
  deviators trigger the predicted "dump everything into the CPR" punishment; subjects
  overtly rejected it ("We screw ourselves, too"), and some *reduced* investment after
  seeing defection.
- **Verdict on Hobbes.** "Covenants, even without a sword, have some force, while swords
  without a covenant may be worse than the state of nature. Best of all the conditions we
  examined are covenants with an internal sword, freely chosen." Self-governance is
  possible — but not guaranteed ("frequently — but not always").

## Limitations
- **Stated:** cannot replace "no cooperation" with a determinate "always cooperate"
  prediction — self-governance is contingent. Findings sit uneasily with standard theory
  (subgame consistency predicts *no* treatment effect), raising the open question of how
  subjects actually conceptualize the task (bounded rationality; subjective continuation
  probability). Small numbers of groups per cell (e.g. 3–4 in some sanctioning-choice
  designs).
- **Design scope:** a single quadratic CPR, n = 8, experienced undergraduate subjects,
  face-to-face (rich, costless, unrestricted-content) communication, anonymous decisions,
  finite horizon. Only *internal* enforcement studied — the authors deliberately did
  **not** test an assigned external monitor/sanctioner (note 24). Communication content
  was constrained (no side payments/threats). Results shown to depend on endowment
  (disequilibrium stakes), so exact percentages are environment-specific.

## Relevance to This Project
This is the canonical human-subjects experiment on *exactly* our E6/E7 question — does
communication alone help, does sanctioning alone help, does communication + sanctioning
help most — and it is the closest empirical analog we have. The mapping is illuminating
precisely where it **disagrees** with our abstract model.

- **Communication alone: they find it protects the RESOURCE; our E6 finds it protects
  FAIRNESS, not the resource.** This is a genuine disagreement, and the reason is
  structural. Ostrom/Walker/Gardner's "communication" is **not** what our model calls
  communication. Their subjects *talk, form an explicit agreement* ("everyone put 4
  tokens in"), *promise*, and *verbally sanction* anonymous defectors — communication is
  already bundled with **coordination and a proto-covenant**. Our E6 broadcast is a bare
  aggregate signal (total harvest last round) decoupled from any agreement, and the
  agent's *response* to it is **retaliation**, which (as in E2) collapses the pool. So
  E6's "communication saves fairness but not the resource" is not a contradiction of
  Ostrom — it is what you get when you strip communication down to information delivery
  and pair it with a resource-destroying response. Their result implies our E6 understates
  what communication can do *because our communication cannot coordinate*.
- **Sanctioning alone: both find it helps the resource, but they add the cost caveat our
  E7 idealizes away.** E7's `enforce` (a binding cap, "any one monitor enforces fully")
  holds sustainability ≥ 0.50 and is the fairest mechanism. Ostrom/Walker/Gardner agree
  the sword raises gross yield (−38% → 37%) — but net of real sanctioning costs it drops
  to **9%**, with overuse, blind revenge, and error. Our clean, costless enforcer flatters
  the result (ADR-0005 already flags this); their data is the empirical warning that
  *real* peer sanctioning without communication is inefficient and error-prone. This
  sharpens, not overturns, E7: enforcement works, but *costless* enforcement is the
  optimistic bound.
- **Communication + sanctioning: their best cell is exactly our proposed follow-up.** The
  design that dominates everything — **one-shot communication + an endogenously chosen
  sword** (93% yield, 90% net, 4% defection) — is precisely the "communication that
  coordinates a *binding agreement*" mechanism our E6/E7 follow-ups name (a consented
  per-capita quota plus the means to uphold it). E7 concludes only a *binding constraint*
  protects both resource and fairness, and that reactive peer responses do not; Ostrom
  et al. supply the human-subjects confirmation *and* the missing ingredient our E6
  lacked: the binding-ness comes from an **agreement reached by communication**, not from
  an externally imposed rule. Their finding that consented enforcement "approximates the
  conditions of a cooperative game" is the strongest available evidence that our
  binding-agreement follow-up should move sustainability into E7's "good corner" — and
  that communication alone (E6-style) is the weakest of the three, communication+consented
  sword the strongest.

**Net implication for our direction:** E6/E7's "communication informs but does not
coordinate" is *correct for cheap-talk-decoupled-from-response*, but Ostrom/Walker/Gardner
show that human communication empirically *does* coordinate because it produces
agreements and (chosen) enforcement. The right follow-up is therefore not "more reliable
signals" but a **collective-choice / binding-agreement channel**: agents use communication
to agree a quota and (optionally, by vote) fund a sanction. Their numbers predict this
should beat both E6-communication and imposed E7-enforcement on the joint
resource+fairness objective.

## Important Terms
- **Covenant** (Hobbes) — a contract involving a promise of future performance; "covenant
  of mutual trust" when both sides so promise. Here: the non-binding verbal agreement
  reached in communication rounds.
- **Sword / external sword vs. internal sword** — enforcement power. *External* = a
  Leviathan/outside agent; *internal* = appropriators sanctioning each other. The paper's
  best outcomes use an **internal, endogenously chosen** sword.
- **Common-pool resource (CPR)** — resource with *subtractable* yield and *nontrivial
  (costly) exclusion*.
- **Subgame-consistent / subgame-perfect equilibrium** — the refined finite-repetition
  equilibrium; here identical across all treatments (≈ 39% net yield), which is why the
  observed treatment effects are anomalous under theory.
- **Rent dissipation** — CPR investment beyond the maximum-net level, wasting the surplus
  (our "over-usage").
- **Trigger strategy / dire threat** — the folk-theorem punishment (all dump into the CPR
  + fines) that supports cooperative imperfect equilibria in principle but was **never
  observed** in the lab.
- **Defection rate** — share of decisions exceeding the agreed investment.
- **Endowment treatment (low/high, 10/25 tokens)** — a *disequilibrium-stakes* knob: same
  equilibrium, different capacity to over-invest and disrupt. Analogous to our
  greed/endowment scaling of how fast a few free-riders can wreck the pool.
- **Endogenous sanctioning** — subjects *vote* whether to institute the sword — the direct
  analog of a collective-choice / binding-agreement mechanism (Ostrom 1990, principle 3).

## Questions
- Their communication-alone success rests on **face-to-face, unrestricted, costless**
  discussion that yields an explicit agreement. Our binding-agreement follow-up must
  decide how much of that to model: just a shared quota target, or also promise/verbal-
  sanction dynamics? How much of the 55–99% gain is the *agreement* vs. the *ongoing
  ability to re-discuss and verbally sanction* (one-shot decayed to 55%; repeated held at
  99%)?
- The high-vs-low endowment gap (73% vs 99% under identical equilibria) says a few big
  investors can defeat communication. Does our simulation reproduce this "a few
  free-riders break the covenant" threshold (cf. E7's collapse at *n_selfish* = 1)? Their
  data suggests communication+consent should *raise* that threshold, not just help
  fairness.
- Sanctioning-alone netted only **9%** after costs. Should E3/E7 add an explicit
  **sanction cost** (fees/fines burn resources) to test whether our "enforcement is best"
  survives realistic costs — and whether communication is what makes the sword *cheap*
  (defection fell to 1–4% with communication, so almost no fines were actually paid)?
- The hysteresis result (prior punitive experience → vote *against* the sword) is a
  path-dependence effect our stateless agents cannot show. Worth flagging as out-of-scope,
  or a motivation for agents with institutional memory?
- Verify secondary numbers against the companion papers (Walker, Gardner & Ostrom 1990,
  1991; Ostrom & Walker 1991) before citing the exact per-round-block figures in the
  thesis.
